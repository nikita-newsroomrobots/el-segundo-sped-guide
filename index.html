Decision Guide: Which Path Is Right for Your Child?

🧭 Understanding Support Levels

ESUSD's ability to serve your child depends heavily on their support level. Here is an honest assessment:

Level 1 / Milder Support Needs

  • Can participate in a general education classroom with accommodations and pull-out services
  • May need support with social skills, sensory processing, executive functioning
  • Can manage transitions with advance notice and visual supports
  • ESUSD is likely adequate. The small district means teachers will know your child personally. The community is engaged.

Level 2-3 / Significant Support Needs

  • Needs dedicated autism classroom (SDC) or intensive behavioral support
  • May need 1:1 aide, BCBA consultation, specialized communication support
  • Significant challenges with general education environment even with supports
  • ESUSD may struggle. The district does not appear to operate dedicated autism classrooms. Its small size limits specialized programs. The district would likely need to place your child at a non-public school (NPS) at district expense, or you will need to advocate aggressively.

📌 Your Path Based on Support Level

If Your Child Has Milder Needs (Level 1)

  1. Enroll in ESUSD — ask the Director of Special Education which campus has the stronger SpEd team
  2. Submit a written assessment request immediately (template in Rights section)
  3. Build a strong IEP with autism-specific goals (see IEP Toolkit)
  4. Layer services: school IEP + Regional Center + insurance-funded ABA
  5. Monitor closely during transitions (especially to middle school)
  6. If progress stalls, request an IEE and consider more intensive supports

If Your Child Has Significant Needs (Level 2-3)

  1. Contact ESUSD SpEd Director and ask directly: “Do you have an autism-specific SDC? Do you employ BCBAs?”
  2. If the answer is no — ask about NPS placement options (district pays)
  3. Consult a special education attorney or advocate BEFORE the first IEP meeting
  4. Consider NPS schools: The Help Group, Kayne ERAS, Carousel School (see Alternatives section)
  5. Contact Harbor Regional Center immediately for additional services
  6. If the district resists appropriate placement, know your dispute resolution options (see Rights section)

👥 Southwest SELPA — What Is It and How It Works

A Special Education Local Plan Area (SELPA) is a consortium of school districts that jointly provide special education services. California requires every district to belong to a SELPA. The SELPA determines how funding is allocated, coordinates regionalized programs, and files performance reports with CDE.

ESUSD is a member of the Southwest SELPA, a multi-district SELPA administered by LACOE serving 12 school districts and 5 charter schools with over 13,000 students with disabilities.

Administering AgencyLos Angeles County Office of Education (LACOE)
Address10322 Condon Avenue, Lennox, CA 90304
Phone(310) 680-5770
Websiteswselpa.org

Advantages

  • Access to regionalized programs operated across member districts
  • SELPA Family Resource Center provides parent support and workshops
  • Community Advisory Committee (CAC) gives parents a voice in policy
  • If ESUSD cannot serve your child's needs, placement in another member district may be possible
  • Program specialists available to consult on IEP development and placement
  • Alternative Dispute Resolution (ADR) services may be available

Limitations

  • ESUSD is a small district within the SELPA — limited in-house specialized programs
  • Funding allocated through SELPA formula; small districts may receive less flexibility
  • Autism-specific programs (dedicated autism SDCs, BCBA staffing) may not exist in-district

Member districts: Centinela Valley Union HSD, El Segundo USD, Hawthorne SD, Hermosa Beach City SD, Inglewood USD, Lawndale Elementary SD, Lennox SD, Manhattan Beach USD, Palos Verdes Peninsula USD, Redondo Beach USD, Torrance USD, and Wiseburn USD.

What Different SELPAs Mean

El Segundo is in the Southwest SELPA (LACOE-administered), while Manhattan Beach, Hermosa Beach, and Redondo Beach are in the South Bay Consortium SELPA. Torrance has its own single-district SELPA. This means:

  • Funding formulas differ between SELPAs
  • Available regionalized programs differ
  • Complaint and dispute resolution processes may vary
  • If considering inter-district transfer, SELPA boundaries affect service delivery

OAH Case 2017080191 — ESUSD v. Parent (2017)

Again district-filed (expedited due process) involving behavioral conduct and educational placement.

Pattern Analysis

  • May indicate ESUSD is proactive about assessment and placement but uses the legal process when parents disagree.
  • Many disputes are settled before hearing — public decisions represent only a fraction of actual disputes.

🔍 Specialized Programs — What to Investigate

ESUSD's website references “General Information and Specialized Programs” though specific autism-focused program details are not prominently published. Ask these questions directly:

  1. Does ESUSD operate any autism-specific special day classes (SDCs)?
  2. Does the district employ BCBAs (Board Certified Behavior Analysts)?
  3. What related services are available (speech, OT, counseling, adaptive PE)?
  4. What inclusion support is available (1:1 aides, push-in specialists)?
  5. What is the district's approach to behavioral support (FBAs, BIPs)?

Middle school is widely recognized as the most challenging period for students on the autism spectrum:

  • Multiple teachers and classrooms — loss of single-classroom structure
  • Changing class periods — navigating hallways, lockers, schedules
  • Heightened social complexity — cliques, hierarchies, puberty
  • Less adult supervision compared to elementary
  • Bullying peaks in middle school; autistic students disproportionately targeted
  • Academic demands increase — more homework, abstract thinking

Potential Positives

  • Only one middle school, so peers from both elementary schools converge — maintains some familiar relationships
  • Mid-size (~700 students) is not as overwhelming as a large middle school
  • Academic performance is reasonable (73% reading, 65% math)

Likely Gaps

  • No autism-specific social skills group (unless written into IEP)
  • Limited structured lunch/recess support
  • General ed teachers with 150+ students may not provide individualized attention
  • Bullying intervention may be reactive rather than preventive

Critical IEP Considerations for Middle School (7 Items)

  1. Transition plan should begin in 5th grade — visit the middle school, practice the routine
  2. Request an aide or peer buddy for the first weeks/months
  3. Ensure social skills instruction is in the IEP
  4. Request a safe space/calm room for breaks or regulation
  5. Request organizational support (executive functioning coaching, check-ins)
  6. Discuss anti-bullying protections and how the school handles social conflicts
  7. Consider whether a smaller class setting or modified schedule is appropriate

🎯 Scenario Analysis — Best / Realistic / Worst Case

Elementary Level Entry (K-5)

Best Case

  • Placed at whichever elementary has strongest SpEd team. Inclusive education with pull-out services. Develops friendships with social skills instruction. 1:1 aide or behavioral support if needed. Small school means teachers know her and advocate for her.

Realistic Case

  • Elementary experience is adequate but not specialized. Gen ed teachers do their best but have limited autism training. Related services (speech, OT) provided per IEP. Social struggles begin but are manageable. Transition to middle school will be the biggest concern.

Worst Case

  • School lacks autism expertise. Needs exceed what gen ed with minimal support can provide. Behavioral challenges or social isolation emerge. District offers a pull-out program that is not autism-specific. You may need NPS placement or an IEE to document the mismatch.

Middle School Entry (6-8)

Best Case

  • Careful transition planning (starting in 5th grade), dedicated case manager, strong IEP supports, social skills group. Finds a peer group through shared interests (clubs, electives).

Realistic Case

  • Transition is bumpy. Social challenges intensify. Academic demands require more support. School provides IEP services but environment not designed for autistic students. Anxiety may increase. You advocate hard and IEP is adjusted.

Worst Case

  • Social dynamics overwhelm her. Bullying or exclusion occurs. Behavioral support insufficient. Anxiety or school refusal develops. NPS placement becomes necessary.

High School Entry (9-12)

Best Case

  • Strong academics provide good college prep pathway. Transition planning begins early. Self-advocacy skills develop. Electives match interests. Graduates with diploma and post-secondary plan.

Realistic Case

  • Large school is challenging but manageable with supports. Transition planning is adequate. Social life limited but has small friend group. Needs ongoing counseling and executive functioning support.

Worst Case

  • Campus size is overwhelming. Social isolation increases. Without strong self-advocacy, services may be inadequate. Consider Da Vinci Schools or NPS as alternatives.

Compliance & Data

📊 California Monitoring Framework

CDE monitors district compliance with IDEA through four mechanisms:

  1. Annual Determinations: CDE issues determinations for each district/SELPA (Meets Requirements, Needs Assistance, Needs Intervention, Needs Substantial Intervention)
  2. Compliance Reviews: Periodic on-site or desk reviews of IEP files and procedures
  3. Disproportionality Reviews: Analysis of whether certain racial/ethnic groups are over- or under-identified for special education
  4. State Complaints: Formal complaints filed with CDE alleging IDEA violations

Common Compliance Challenges for Small Districts Like ESUSD

  • Timely completion of evaluations (60-day timeline)
  • Transition planning at age 16 (fewer vocational options)
  • LRE percentages — fewer inclusion supports may lead to more restrictive placements
  • Related services staffing — difficulty recruiting/retaining BCBAs, OTs, SLPs with autism expertise

📚 Charter School Considerations

Charter schools are public schools and must provide special education services under IDEA. However, quality varies greatly.

Key Questions to Ask Any Charter School

  • Is the charter school its own LEA for SpEd, or does it operate under its authorizing district's SELPA?
  • What specific SpEd staff do you employ (SLPs, OTs, school psychologists, BCBAs)?
  • How are IEPs implemented in your school environment?
  • What class sizes and support staff ratios exist?

Nearby Charter Options

  • Da Vinci Schools — Hawthorne/El Segundo; 9-12; project-based learning (see School Guide above)
  • Environmental Charter Schools — Lawndale/Gardena; middle & high school; project-based learning may benefit visual/hands-on learners
  • Wiseburn Da Vinci — El Segundo area; partnership between Wiseburn USD and Da Vinci Schools

Important Note

  • For children with significant autism-related needs, charter schools may not have the intensive support infrastructure (dedicated autism classrooms, BCBAs, 1:1 aides) needed. They work best for students who can function in gen ed with some accommodations.

Lanterman Act Rights

Under the Lanterman Developmental Disabilities Services Act, individuals with developmental disabilities (including autism) have a right to services that help them live as independently and normally as possible. The Regional Center cannot deny services solely due to budget constraints if those services are needed.

Key Point

  • Regional Center services are separate from and in addition to school district services. A child can have both an IEP and Regional Center services simultaneously.
  • Regional Center covers evenings, weekends, summers, and services the school doesn't provide.

🛡️ Insurance Coverage & Medi-Cal

California Autism Insurance Mandate (SB 946)

California law requires state-regulated health insurance plans to cover behavioral health treatment for autism:

  • No age cap on coverage
  • No dollar cap on coverage
  • Requires a diagnosis of ASD and a prescription/referral from a physician
  • Covers ABA therapy and other evidence-based behavioral interventions
  • A qualified BCBA must design and supervise treatment

Important: Check Your Plan Type

  • Self-funded employer plans (ERISA) are not subject to state mandates — check if your plan is state-regulated or self-funded
  • Insurance-funded ABA and school-based services are not mutually exclusive — your child can receive both

Medi-Cal Coverage

If income-eligible, Medi-Cal covers ABA and behavioral health through the Early Periodic Screening, Diagnosis and Treatment (EPSDT) benefit. Medi-Cal has its own ABA coverage separate from the SB 946 mandate.

Your Rights & Advocacy

📜 Core Federal Rights (IDEA)

  • Right to FAPE: Free Appropriate Public Education at no cost, with “meaningful educational benefit”
  • Right to Participate: You are an equal IEP team member. Bring anyone with knowledge of your child (34 C.F.R. §300.321(a)(6))
  • Prior Written Notice (PWN): District must notify you before proposing/refusing any change (34 C.F.R. §300.503)
  • Informed Consent: Required before evaluation, initial placement, and reevaluation
  • Access Records: Right to inspect all education records within 45 days (34 C.F.R. §300.613)
  • Procedural Safeguards: Provided at least once per year and upon request (34 C.F.R. §300.504)

⚖️ Endrew F. — The “Meaningful Benefit” Standard

In Endrew F. v. Douglas County School District (2017), the U.S. Supreme Court ruled that an IEP must be “reasonably calculated to enable a child to make progress appropriate in light of the child’s circumstances.”

What This Means for You

  • Your child is entitled to more than minimal progress — the old “some benefit” standard is dead
  • The IEP must be designed for meaningful educational benefit
  • If the district proposes an IEP that only maintains current levels without growth, it likely violates Endrew F.
  • Use this phrase: “Under Endrew F., my child is entitled to meaningful educational benefit, not just some benefit.”

Citation: Endrew F. v. Douglas County (2017), 137 S. Ct. 988

📋 Placement Continuum & the Holland Test (LRE)

The Full Continuum of Placements (Cal. Ed. Code §56360)

  1. General education classroom with supplementary aids, services, and supports
  2. General education with pull-out services (resource room for some subjects)
  3. Special day class (SDC) on a general education campus (with mainstreaming opportunities)
  4. Special day class on a separate campus
  5. Non-public school (NPS) — private school for SWDs, funded by district
  6. Residential placement
  7. Home/hospital instruction

The Rachel H. / Holland Test (9th Circuit LRE Standard)

In California, courts use the four-factor test from Sacramento City USD v. Rachel H. (1994):

  1. Educational benefits of gen ed with supplementary aids vs. special ed placement
  2. Non-academic benefits (social interaction, role modeling) of gen ed
  3. Effect on the teacher and other students in gen ed
  4. Cost (only if gen ed would be significantly more expensive)

Key Points for Autism Placements

  • District cannot place your child in a more restrictive setting just because it's more convenient
  • Supplementary aids and services must be tried and found inadequate before moving to more restrictive placement
  • An autistic child who benefits socially from inclusion may need to be in gen ed even if academics require significant modification
  • Placement must be an individualized determination — no one-size-fits-all

Citation: Sacramento City USD v. Rachel H. (1994), 14 F.3d 1398

🌸 California-Specific Rights (Extra Protections)

  • 60-day assessment timeline after consent (not counting breaks >5 days) — Cal. Ed. Code §56344(a)
  • Assessment Plan within 15 days of your written referral — Cal. Ed. Code §56321(a)
  • 10-day advance notice of IEP meetings — Cal. Ed. Code §56341.5(c)
  • Audio recording rights — give 24-hour notice to record IEP meetings — Cal. Ed. Code §56341.1(g)
  • Stay-put rights — child stays in current placement during disputes — 20 U.S.C. §1415(j)
  • Translation & interpretation in your primary language — Cal. Ed. Code §56341.5(i)
  • ESY consideration if regression risk during breaks — Cal. Ed. Code §56345(b)(3)
  • 30-day IEP meeting upon your written request — Cal. Ed. Code §56343.5
  • Surrogate parents appointed for wards of the state

California Autism Definition for SpEd

“Autism means a developmental disability significantly affecting verbal and nonverbal communication and social interaction, generally evident before age three, that adversely affects a child’s educational performance.” — Cal. Ed. Code §56846.2; 5 CCR §3030(b)(1)

Important: A child does not need a medical diagnosis to qualify under the IDEA autism category. The educational team makes its own determination. Conversely, a medical diagnosis does not automatically guarantee eligibility.

📋 IEP Process Step-by-Step

  1. Referral (Child Find) — Submit written referral to school principal or SpEd director. Under IDEA's Child Find obligation, the district must identify, locate, and evaluate all children with suspected disabilities.
  2. Assessment Plan — District provides within 15 days. Review carefully — ensure it covers all areas: cognitive, academic, speech/language (including pragmatic), social-emotional, adaptive behavior, sensory processing, motor skills, behavioral assessment.
  3. Consent & Evaluation — Sign plan (you have at least 15 days to review). District has 60 days to complete all assessments and hold IEP meeting.
  4. Eligibility Determination — IEP team (including you) determines autism category eligibility and whether disability adversely affects educational performance.
  5. IEP Development — Present levels (PLAAFP), measurable goals, services, accommodations, behavioral support, transition planning.
  6. Placement Decision — Determined AFTER goals and services — never the reverse. Full continuum considered in LRE.
  7. Implementation — District implements as written. You receive progress reports at least as often as report cards.
  8. Annual Review — At least yearly; you can request meetings anytime (30 days to respond).
  9. Triennial Reevaluation — Every 3 years; you can request reevaluation anytime (once per year without district agreement).

🔨 IEE Request Letter Template

If you disagree with any district evaluation, you can request an Independent Educational Evaluation at public expense (the district pays).

[Date] [Director of Special Education] [District Name] [Address] Re: Request for Independent Educational Evaluation at Public Expense Student: [Child's Name] DOB: [Date of Birth] Dear [Director], I am writing to request an Independent Educational Evaluation (IEE) at public expense for my child, [Child's Name], pursuant to 34 C.F.R. Section 300.502. I disagree with the district's [type of evaluation, e.g., psychoeducational assessment, speech-language evaluation] dated [date of district evaluation]. Please provide me with the district's IEE criteria, including any approved evaluator lists and cost guidelines, within a reasonable timeframe. Key IEE Rights

  • District must either fund the IEE or file for due process to prove their evaluation was appropriate. They cannot simply refuse.
  • One IEE at public expense per district evaluation you disagree with
  • You can always get an IEE at your own expense — the IEP team must consider it
  • Simply state disagreement; you do NOT need to explain why

💡 Practical Advocacy Tips

Before the IEP Meeting

  • Read Disability Rights CA “Special Education Rights and Responsibilities” guide (free)
  • Organize records in a binder (assessments, IEPs, correspondence, medical records, work samples)
  • Write a Parent Concerns letter to be included in the IEP
  • Bring someone with you for note-taking and support
  • Request draft IEP and all reports at least 2 days before the meeting
  • Prepare a list of questions and requests

During the IEP Meeting

  • Record the meeting (24-hour advance notice)
  • Use the phrase “I want that in the IEP” when team agrees verbally
  • Ask for data when district says “making progress”
  • Don't be rushed — continue on another date if needed
  • Don't sign if unsure — take it home to review
  • If district says “we don't do that” — that is NOT a legal justification
  • Request Prior Written Notice for any proposal/refusal you disagree with
  • Focus on your child's needs, not labels or programs

After the IEP Meeting

  • Send follow-up email summarizing agreements (“per our conversation today…”)
  • Monitor implementation — check services are actually being provided
  • Track progress; request meetings whenever you have concerns
  • Everything in writing — document, document, document

“What to Say When the District Says No”

  • “Please provide me with Prior Written Notice explaining why you are refusing this request.”
  • “Under Endrew F., my child is entitled to meaningful educational benefit.”
  • “What data supports the current program is providing meaningful benefit?”
  • “I'd like that concern noted in the IEP document.”
  • “I disagree and am reserving my rights to seek dispute resolution.”

IEP Toolkit: Goals, Accommodations & Services

🎯 Sample IEP Goals by Area

Use these as starting points when developing IEP goals. All goals should be individualized to your child.

Social Communication (4 goals)

  • [Child] will initiate a social interaction with a peer (greeting, asking to play, commenting) independently in 4 out of 5 opportunities across settings.
  • [Child] will maintain a reciprocal conversation of 3+ exchanges on a topic of mutual interest with a peer in 4 out of 5 opportunities.
  • [Child] will identify and respond appropriately to nonverbal social cues (facial expressions, body language, tone of voice) in 4 out of 5 observed opportunities.
  • [Child] will use a repair strategy (asking for clarification, rephrasing) when a communication breakdown occurs in 3 out of 4 opportunities.

Pragmatic Language (2 goals)

  • [Child] will use appropriate topic maintenance, turn-taking, and active listening skills during group discussions in 4 out of 5 opportunities.
  • [Child] will demonstrate understanding of figurative language (idioms, metaphors, sarcasm) by correctly interpreting meaning in 4 out of 5 presented examples.

Emotional Regulation (3 goals)

  • [Child] will independently identify and label their emotional state using a visual scale or tool in 4 out of 5 opportunities.
  • [Child] will use a pre-taught coping strategy (deep breathing, requesting a break, calm-down space) when experiencing frustration or anxiety, reducing [target behavior] from [baseline] to [target].
  • [Child] will independently transition between activities/settings with no more than one verbal prompt in 4 out of 5 transitions.

Executive Functioning (3 goals)

  • [Child] will independently use a visual schedule or planner to organize and complete daily tasks in 4 out of 5 school days.
  • [Child] will break a multi-step assignment into component parts and complete each step with no more than one prompt in 3 out of 4 opportunities.
  • [Child] will independently initiate and complete a classroom assignment within the expected timeframe in 4 out of 5 opportunities.

Sensory Processing (2 goals)

  • [Child] will independently use a sensory strategy (sensory break, fidget tool, noise-canceling headphones) to maintain regulation during classroom activities for [duration].
  • [Child] will request a sensory break or accommodation when feeling overwhelmed, rather than [maladaptive behavior], in 4 out of 5 opportunities.

Adaptive Behavior / Daily Living (2 goals)

  • [Child] will independently manage personal belongings (backpack, materials) upon arrival and dismissal in 4 out of 5 school days.
  • [Child] will independently navigate the lunch routine (ordering/unpacking, eating, cleaning up) in 4 out of 5 school days.

Academic Considerations

  • Reading comprehension: Inference, perspective-taking in literature
  • Written expression: Organizing ideas, elaborating, flexible thinking
  • Math problem-solving: Word problems, showing work, flexible strategies

📋 Accommodations Checklist by Category

Environmental Accommodations (6)

  • Preferential seating (away from distractions, near teacher, or per student preference)
  • Reduced visual and auditory clutter in the classroom
  • Access to a quiet/calm space for breaks or regulation
  • Noise-canceling headphones or earplugs
  • Flexible seating options (wobble stool, standing desk)
  • Consistent, predictable classroom routines

Instructional Accommodations (11)

  • Visual schedules and advance notice of schedule changes
  • Visual supports (graphic organizers, visual timers, written instructions)
  • Chunking of assignments and instructions
  • Extended time for assignments and tests
  • Reduced homework load or modified assignments (if appropriate)
  • Advance notice of transitions with countdowns or timers
  • Priming/pre-teaching of new material
  • Copy of teacher notes or guided notes
  • Preferential grouping for cooperative learning activities
  • Check-ins for comprehension
  • Multiple means of demonstrating knowledge

Social/Behavioral Supports (10)

  • Social skills instruction (individual or small group)
  • Social narratives (Social Stories) for challenging situations
  • Structured/facilitated peer interactions (lunch bunch, buddy system)
  • Access to a trusted adult for check-ins
  • Behavior Intervention Plan (BIP) based on Functional Behavior Assessment
  • Positive behavior support strategies
  • Token economy or reinforcement system aligned with interests
  • Sensory diet developed by occupational therapist
  • De-escalation plan for crisis situations
  • Anti-bullying supports and social monitoring

Communication Supports (5)

  • Visual communication supports (picture schedules, communication boards)
  • Augmentative and Alternative Communication (AAC) devices if needed
  • Extra processing time for verbal instructions
  • Written or visual instructions in addition to verbal
  • Social scripts for common situations

Assessment Accommodations (6)

  • Extended time on tests
  • Testing in a separate/small group setting
  • Breaks during testing
  • Read-aloud for non-reading tests
  • Use of calculator or other tools as appropriate
  • Alternative response formats

✅ Related Services Checklist (9 Services)

Consider whether each of these should be in your child's IEP:

  1. Speech-Language Therapy (individual and/or group, targeting pragmatic language)
  2. Occupational Therapy (sensory processing, fine motor, self-regulation)
  3. Applied Behavior Analysis (ABA) services or behavioral support
  4. Counseling (individual or group — anxiety, social skills, self-advocacy)

🏫 Per-School Impact Narratives

Elementary (K-5): What Your Daughter's Experience Will Likely Look Like

If she has mild-moderate support needs: She will likely be placed in gen ed with an IEP. Pull-out services (speech, OT) provided per IEP. May receive RSP support. A 1:1 aide may be provided if documented. Small school environment is positive — teachers will know her. Social challenges will emerge but are more manageable in elementary. Main risk: Teachers may not have deep autism expertise; support may be well-intentioned but generic.

If she has significant support needs: District may not have an appropriate in-house placement. May be placed in an SDC that is not autism-specific. Real possibility of NPS placement (Kayne ERAS, Carousel School, The Help Group). NPS means leaving the neighborhood school but receiving specialized instruction. Main risk: Inappropriate placement in a program not designed for autism; insufficient behavioral support.

Recommendation: Center Street may be slightly better due to smaller size and STEM focus. But ask the Director of Special Education which campus has the stronger SpEd team.

Middle School (6-8): Highest-Risk Transition Period

Likely experience: The shift from one teacher to multiple will be disorienting. Social dynamics intensify — cliques form, bullying peaks. Executive functioning demands increase. Sensory overload from hallway transitions, crowded lunch. If stable in elementary, middle school may destabilize things.

Likely supports available: IEP accommodations, pull-out services, possibly a resource class period.

Likely gaps: No autism-specific social skills group (unless in IEP), limited structured lunch support, gen ed teachers with 150+ students, reactive bullying intervention.

Recommendation: Begin transition planning in 4th-5th grade. Request a formal transition IEP. If she struggles significantly, seriously consider NPS or smaller alternative. Monitor closely during 6th grade.

High School (9-12): ESHS vs. Da Vinci

At El Segundo High: Academically strong (#105 in CA) — good for college-bound autistic students. Largest school (1,290) — most demanding socially/sensorily. Transition planning required by 16. Electives may match interests. Self-advocacy becomes critical.

At Da Vinci Schools: Project-based learning may be better for hands-on learners. Smaller environments. RISE specifically serves students who struggled in traditional settings. Has full SpEd continuum. Contact: Dr. Lynn Rodriguez, lrodriguez@davincischools.org

Recommendation: If academically strong and socially coping, ESHS can work with robust IEP. If traditional environment is overwhelming, Da Vinci is a compelling local alternative. For specialized therapeutic environment, NPS remains an option (The Help Group's Village Glen or STEM3 Academy).

🚨 Red Flags & Green Flags

Red Flags to Watch For

  • “We don't have a program for that” — Not a legal justification. District must provide what your child needs.
  • One-size-fits-all IEP — If it doesn't specifically address autism-related needs, push back.
  • Resistance to assessments — Demand Prior Written Notice if they refuse.
  • Offering only what they have — IEP must drive placement, not the reverse.
  • Pushing restrictive placement without trying supports first
  • No data on progress — If they can't show data, that's a problem.

Green Flags

  • Trained staff with autism-specific expertise
  • BCBA on staff or contracted
  • Willingness to assess comprehensively
  • Collaboration with outside providers
  • Individualized IEP tailored to your child's specific needs
  • Data-driven progress monitoring with regular collection
